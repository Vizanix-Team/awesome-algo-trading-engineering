## 1. Power Concentration as an Economic, Not Just Political, Question

Discussions of "AI and power" often jump straight to dramatic political scenarios, authoritarian surveillance states, corporate monopolies, geopolitical arms races, without first establishing the more basic economic mechanism that makes all of those downstream scenarios possible in the first place: AI development currently exhibits strong economies of scale, meaning the most capable systems require capital, computational infrastructure, and specialized talent at a scale accessible only to a small number of well-resourced actors, a pattern quite different from software development generally, where a small team with modest resources has often been able to compete with much larger incumbents.

This scale requirement is the structural root of every power-concentration concern that follows in this essay, and it is worth being precise about why it exists rather than treating it as an inherent, unchangeable property of AI as a technology. Training the most capable current-generation models requires computational infrastructure costing hundreds of millions to billions of dollars, energy infrastructure to power that computation, and access to talent pools that remain genuinely scarce relative to demand. None of these three inputs is evenly distributed globally or even within wealthy economies, and each creates a natural chokepoint where a relatively small number of actors, whether corporations, well-funded research labs, or state actors with sufficient resources, can exert outsized influence over the trajectory of the technology relative to their share of the population or the economy.

It is worth noting, without resolving the question, that this scale-driven concentration is not necessarily permanent. Compute costs per unit of capability have historically fallen over time as hardware and algorithmic efficiency improve, a pattern that, if it continues, could gradually lower the barrier to entry and diffuse capability more broadly over a period of years, echoing how earlier waves of computing hardware started concentrated in a handful of institutions and eventually became widely accessible. Whether AI capability follows a similar democratizing trajectory or remains persistently concentrated because the capability frontier keeps moving faster than the cost curve falls is one of the central open questions this essay returns to throughout.

## 2. The Compute and Data Bottlenecks

Two inputs deserve particular attention because they function as genuine physical and legal bottlenecks rather than merely organizational ones: computational hardware and training data.

Advanced AI training depends on specialized processors manufactured by a very small number of companies, using fabrication facilities that are themselves concentrated in a handful of countries and require enormous, multi-year capital investment to build. This creates a supply chain with genuine chokepoints that function similarly to how, in a much earlier era, control over strategic raw materials shaped industrial and geopolitical power, though the analogy should not be pushed too far since semiconductor manufacturing capacity, unlike a mineral deposit, can in principle be built anywhere sufficient capital, expertise, and time are invested, making the concentration a function of accumulated industrial capability and specialized expertise rather than fixed geological luck.

Training data presents a different kind of bottleneck, legal and normative rather than purely physical. The most capable current models are trained on enormous corpora scraped from publicly available internet content, a practice whose legal status under copyright and data protection law remains genuinely unsettled in multiple jurisdictions as of this writing, and whose long-term availability is uncertain as content platforms increasingly restrict scraping access, either to protect their own commercial interests in licensing that data or in response to creator objections about uncompensated use. If large-scale public web data becomes harder to access, whether through legal restriction or platform-level technical barriers, this would tend to further advantage large incumbent firms that have already accumulated substantial proprietary data stores or negotiated exclusive licensing arrangements, reinforcing rather than reducing concentration, an outcome that is not universally intended by data protection advocates but is a plausible unintended consequence worth naming honestly.

## 3. Corporate Concentration: How Unusual Is This, Really

A useful discipline before treating AI-driven corporate concentration as unprecedented is to ask how it compares to concentration patterns in other capital-intensive, scale-driven industries historically: telecommunications infrastructure, commercial aviation, and semiconductor manufacturing itself have all exhibited similarly small numbers of dominant global players for extended periods, driven by comparable economies of scale and high capital barriers to entry, without those industries being considered existential threats to economic pluralism in the way AI concentration sometimes is discussed.

What may be genuinely different about AI, compared to these prior examples, is the breadth of downstream application. A concentrated market for aircraft manufacturing affects aviation; a concentrated market for the foundational technology increasingly embedded across search, communication, software development, scientific research, and decision support systems affects a much wider swath of economic and social activity simultaneously, meaning the stakes of concentration in this specific sector may be proportionally higher even if the underlying economic mechanism, scale economies favoring large incumbents, is not itself novel.

It is also worth noting a countervailing consideration: unlike some historically concentrated industries where the concentrated firms directly controlled a scarce physical resource with no substitute, AI's core outputs, text generation, image generation, code assistance, currently face meaningful competition among several well-resourced firms simultaneously, along with a genuine and increasingly capable open-weight alternative ecosystem discussed later in this essay, producing more competitive pressure at the application layer than the concentration at the infrastructure layer might suggest in isolation. Concentration in who can train frontier models does not automatically translate into equivalent concentration in who can build valuable products and services on top of them, a distinction that matters for how worried one should be about any single layer of the stack.

## 4. State Power: Surveillance, Enforcement, and Military Applications

Separate from corporate concentration is the question of how AI capability shifts the balance of power between states and their citizens, and among states themselves, a question with a much longer and more troubling historical precedent than the corporate concentration question, since every major advance in information-processing and surveillance technology throughout history, from centralized record-keeping to telecommunications interception to digital data aggregation, has expanded the practical capacity of state authorities to monitor and control populations, sometimes for legitimate public safety purposes and sometimes for purposes most observers would consider illegitimate, with the balance depending heavily on the specific institutional and legal context in which the capability is deployed rather than on the technology itself.

AI-enabled surveillance, facial recognition at scale, automated analysis of communications metadata, predictive systems used in law enforcement or resource allocation decisions, extends this long historical pattern rather than introducing a wholly new dynamic, but it does so with a meaningful difference in degree: the cost of comprehensive, persistent monitoring of a large population has fallen dramatically, removing a practical resource constraint, the sheer labor cost of human analysts reviewing footage or records, that historically limited how comprehensively even a determined authority could surveil its population. Where surveillance was once bounded by how many analysts a government could employ, it is increasingly bounded only by computational and data-storage capacity, a much less binding constraint.

Military applications raise a related but distinct concern, centered less on population monitoring and more on decision-speed and autonomy in the use of force. Systems that assist with target identification, logistics optimization, or autonomous weapons platforms introduce a genuine, actively debated policy question about how much decision authority over lethal force should be delegated to automated systems, a question that intersects with the broader alignment and reliability concerns discussed elsewhere in this collection, since the consequences of a misaligned or malfunctioning system are considerably higher stakes in a military context than in a commercial one. This essay does not take a position on any specific state's military AI policy, consistent with its analytical rather than advocacy purpose, but it notes that the technical reliability concerns documented in the alignment essay apply with particular force in exactly this domain, where the cost of an error is measured in irreversible harm rather than a bad customer service interaction.

## 5. The International Dimension: Coordination Versus Competition

AI capability has become explicitly linked, in the public statements of numerous governments, to national economic competitiveness and, in some cases, military strategic advantage, producing a dynamic that game theorists would recognize as a classic coordination problem: every individual state may have an interest in some form of shared safety standards or coordinated pacing of the most consequential capability developments, but each also has an individual incentive to defect from any such coordination if doing so grants a competitive advantage, a structure that has historically made this class of problem very difficult to solve durably without either overwhelming shared interest or a hegemonic actor able to enforce cooperation, neither of which reliably characterizes the current international landscape around AI.

This dynamic has real historical precedent, most closely to nuclear technology, where the international community did eventually develop meaningful, if imperfect, coordination mechanisms around nonproliferation and safety norms, but only after a period of intense unilateral competition and only because nuclear weapons possess characteristics, extreme destructive power concentrated in a single, identifiable, hard-to-conceal artifact, that made verification and enforcement of agreements comparatively tractable. AI capability differs in ways that could make analogous coordination harder: it is embedded in general-purpose software rather than a single identifiable physical artifact, its most consequential applications are not always obviously distinguishable from beneficial ones at the point of development, and the underlying knowledge diffuses through research publication and open-source release in ways that resist the kind of centralized custody that made nuclear material more governable.

None of this means international coordination on AI is impossible, only that direct analogy to nuclear governance likely overstates how tractable the verification and enforcement problem will be, and that any effective coordination mechanism will probably need to be structurally different from the nuclear precedent rather than a copy of it, a design challenge that remains substantially unsolved as of this writing, with various partial, non-binding international dialogues underway but no comprehensive framework yet in place that resembles the enforcement mechanisms nuclear nonproliferation eventually developed.

## 6. Checks That Have Worked Before, and Their Limits Here

Societies have developed a range of institutional checks against excessive concentration of power over the centuries, and it is worth examining each honestly for how well it transfers to the AI context rather than assuming either that history offers a ready-made solution or that AI is so novel that no historical check applies at all.

Antitrust and competition law has a long track record of constraining corporate power concentration in other capital-intensive industries, and elements of it plausibly apply to AI infrastructure and platform markets, though enforcement in most jurisdictions has historically moved on a multi-year timescale that may lag well behind how quickly market structure in a fast-moving technology sector can solidify, a mismatch that has drawn genuine concern from competition policy scholars independent of any specific political orientation.

Democratic accountability mechanisms, elections, free press scrutiny, judicial review, function reasonably well at constraining state power when the state's capability is transparent enough for citizens and institutions to observe and evaluate, but surveillance and security-related AI applications are often deployed with limited public visibility by design, for legitimate operational security reasons in some cases and for less legitimate reasons in others, weakening the practical bite of accountability mechanisms that depend on visibility to function.

Technical transparency requirements, model documentation standards, mandated disclosure of training data and capability evaluations, represent a newer category of check specifically adapted to this technology, still in an early and contested stage of development across different jurisdictions, with genuinely unresolved debates about how much disclosure is technically meaningful without simply producing compliance paperwork that does not actually constrain concentrated power in practice.

The honest summary is that every historical check against power concentration has some relevance here, and none transfers cleanly, leaving a genuine institutional design gap that current governance efforts are only beginning to address, with substantial disagreement about which combination of these mechanisms, or entirely new ones not yet developed, will prove adequate.

## 7. The Open-Source Counterweight, Examined Honestly

A frequently cited counterweight to AI power concentration is the open-weight model ecosystem, systems whose trained parameters are published publicly, allowing a much broader range of actors, smaller companies, academic researchers, and individual developers, to build on frontier-adjacent capability without needing to bear the full cost of training from scratch. This ecosystem has grown substantially and has, in specific documented cases, narrowed the capability gap between the most well-resourced labs and a much broader base of users faster than many observers initially expected, providing real evidence that concentration is not an immutable feature of the technology.

A fair-minded skeptic of the open-source counterweight raises two points worth taking seriously. First, even open-weight models still require the same concentrated infrastructure, specialized hardware and large capital outlays, to train in the first place, meaning open release diffuses access to a given capability level after it has been developed, but does not by itself diffuse the capacity to develop the next capability level, leaving the actual frontier of research still concentrated among well-resourced actors even as broader access to already-achieved capability improves. Second, the same broad accessibility that diffuses beneficial uses also diffuses misuse potential, since open-weight release makes safety-relevant restrictions, such as refusing to assist with clearly harmful requests, considerably harder to enforce once a model's weights are in the hands of anyone willing to run them without those restrictions intact, a genuine tradeoff between diffusing power and diffusing risk that does not have a clean resolution.

Both of these considerations are legitimate, and the honest conclusion is that open-weight release is a real, partial counterweight to power concentration at the application and access layer, not a complete solution to concentration at the frontier-research layer, and it introduces its own distinct risk-distribution tradeoffs that a purely enthusiastic account of open source as a power-diffusing force tends to underweight.

## 8. What Durable Distributed Power Might Require

Pulling the threads of this essay together, a durable move away from AI-driven power concentration, if it is to happen, likely requires progress on several fronts simultaneously rather than any single fix: continued reduction in the cost of achieving a given level of capability, which broadens who can meaningfully participate in development rather than only in deployment; competition policy and regulatory frameworks that can move at a speed closer to the technology's own pace than historical antitrust enforcement has managed; international coordination mechanisms structurally adapted to AI's diffuse, software-embedded nature rather than borrowed wholesale from the nuclear precedent; and technical transparency and interpretability tools robust enough to make meaningful public and institutional oversight of powerful systems actually possible rather than merely nominal.

None of these is guaranteed to materialize, and reasonable observers disagree about which is most tractable or most urgent. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that the current trajectory, absent deliberate institutional effort on several of these fronts, leans toward concentration rather than diffusion, given the scale economies described at the outset of this essay, and that recognizing this tendency clearly is a necessary first step toward any serious effort to counteract it, whatever specific combination of measures a given society or institution ultimately judges most appropriate.

## Where This Leaves Us

AI's relationship to power concentration is not a story about a technology developing ambitions of its own, but a familiar story, playing out with unusual speed, about scarce and valuable capabilities tending to accumulate in the hands of whoever can afford the capital, infrastructure, and talent required to develop them, and about whether existing checks, corporate competition law, democratic accountability, international coordination, can adapt quickly enough to matter.

The historical record offers genuine grounds for both concern and cautious hope. Concern, because the scale economies here are real and currently trend toward concentration faster than most existing institutional checks can respond. Hope, because falling capability costs, a genuinely competitive multi-firm landscape at the application layer, and a substantive open-weight ecosystem all provide real, if partial and imperfect, counterweights that previous concentrated technologies did not always have available this early in their development. Which set of forces wins out is not predetermined by the technology itself; it depends on choices, institutional, legal, and political, that are still very much being made.
